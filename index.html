I am a PhD researcher at the University of Oxford, based in the Reasoning with Machines Lab (OxRML) and supervised by Professor Adam Mahdi. I build evaluations and methods that automate scientific research in a safe and translational way.

AI agents (coding, multimodal, physical and more) can now do a great deal. I want to understand which parts of scientific research we can hand over to them, and how to check answers that sound right but have no ground truth to test against. To do this, my work spans evaluation design, post-training and AI safety, often testing multimodal and agentic systems. My research has featured at AI venues such as ICML, NeurIPS and EMNLP.

Branches I am currently interested in:

  • Models that improve models (others or themselves). How AI agents train other models when they must balance several machine learning objectives at once, often competing ones. And how well they do it.
  • Discovery and integrity. Which stages of training decide a model's ethics. Whether models trained without ethical limits make better scientific discoveries. Whether a model could learn to act ethically in training but not at test time.
  • Uncertainty and conflicting evidence. How models represent uncertainty and weigh conflicting information, and how this shapes their conclusions. Do they always take the same scientific route? Can we steer their early calls to prevent failures downstream? [Maybe LLMs cannot, and this is a job for world models?]
  • Broader use of AI in research. Whether automating research helps or harms science overall. [That needs a crystal ball, but smaller use cases can be tested piece by piece.] What balance of human and AI work is best, and what kind of feedback, in what form, gets the best results. [Scale, and confounds from the harness itself, make this hard to answer.]

Research

Selected publications. * Equal contribution. Full list on Google Scholar. Click a figure for a short summary.

  1. Evaluating AI-based Scientific Knowledge Synthesis with Epidemiological Systematic Reviews

    S. Padarha*, R. O. Kearns*, T. Naidoo, L. Yang, Ł. Borchmann, P. Błaszczyk, C. Morgenstern, R. McCabe, S. Bhatia, P. H. Torr, J. Foerster, S. A. Hale, T. Rawson, A. Cori, E. Semenova, A. Mahdi

    NeurIPS 2026 Paper Code Dataset Website

    Evaluating AI-based Scientific Knowledge Synthesis with Epidemiological Systematic Reviews

    TL;DR Five frontier LLMs take on the full systematic-review pipeline for WHO priority pathogens, checked against expert labels on 16,248 articles. The pipeline finishes in about 20 hours against roughly 385 for a manual review, but no model wins every stage and data extraction stays the weak link.

    Key takeaway LLMs can compress weeks of evidence synthesis into a day, but not yet without an expert in the loop.

  2. Agentic Reinforcement Learning for Search Misaligns Instruction-Tuning

    Y. Yang, S. Padarha, S. Ball, A. Lee, A. Mahdi

    EMNLP 2026 Paper Code

    Agentic Reinforcement Learning for Search Misaligns Instruction-Tuning

    TL;DR Train an instruction-tuned model to search with reinforcement learning and its refusals turn brittle: nudge it to search first and it chains harmful queries, cutting search safety by more than two-thirds. The damage traces to one direction inside the model, and penalising it during training restores safety at no cost to accuracy.

    Key takeaway Teaching a model to use tools can quietly undo its safety training, but the fix is cheap.

  3. Strategic Navigation or Stochastic Search? How Agents and Humans Reason Over Document Collections

    Ł. Borchmann, J. Van Landeghem, M. Turski, S. Padarha, R. O. Kearns, A. Mahdi, et al.

    ICML 2026 · Oral, top 0.5% Paper Code Dataset Leaderboard

    Strategic Navigation or Stochastic Search? How Agents and Humans Reason Over Document Collections

    TL;DR The best AI agents now match humans at answering questions across 800 real PDFs. But they get there by brute-force searching rather than planning, solve different questions from people and stall almost 20 percent short of the best possible score.

    Key takeaway Matching human accuracy is not the same as reasoning like a human.

  4. Running the Gauntlet: Challenging Agentic Tasks

    M. Vysotskyi*, R. Lin*, G. Biziel, …, S. Padarha, …, A. Mahdi, V. Karpiv, P. Torr, A. Bibi

    Paper Code Website

    Running the Gauntlet: Challenging Agentic Tasks

    TL;DR Frontier agents were dropped into five unfamiliar professional apps, from a video editor to a circuit designer. At launch the best one solved 28.2 percent of tasks against over 80 percent for non-expert humans; three months later, GPT-6 Astra hit 83.0 percent.

    Key takeaway Agents are learning unfamiliar tools fast, and benchmarks now age in months, not years.

  5. Evaluating LLM-as-a-Judge under Multilingual, Multimodal and Multi-domain Constraints

    S. Padarha, E. Semenova, B. Vidgen, A. Mahdi, S. A. Hale

    NeurIPS 2025 Workshop on Evaluating the Evolving LLM Lifecycle Paper

    Evaluating LLM-as-a-Judge under Multilingual, Multimodal and Multi-domain Constraints

    TL;DR LLM judges, which grade other models during post-training, were tested across 12 languages on image-and-text tasks. They catch hallucinations with up to 95.2 percent accuracy but judge reasoning at close to a coin toss, and the best judge changes with the language and the task.

    Key takeaway There is no universally good judge, so judges should be evaluated by language and task.

  6. Fully Automated Deep Learning Framework for Detection and Classification of Impacted Mandibular Third Molars in Panoramic Radiographs

    S. K. Veerabhadrappa, S. Vengusamy, S. Padarha, K. Iyer, S. Yadav

    Journal of Oral Medicine and Oral Surgery Paper

    Fully Automated Deep Learning Framework for Detection and Classification of Impacted Mandibular Third Molars in Panoramic Radiographs

    TL;DR A three-stage deep learning pipeline reads panoramic X-rays, finds impacted wisdom teeth with 93.5 percent accuracy and classifies their angle with 92.2 percent accuracy. Built with clinicians in Malaysia and the Philippines, it is now used by dental schools.

    Key takeaway A routine, error-prone step in planning wisdom-tooth surgery can be automated reliably.
