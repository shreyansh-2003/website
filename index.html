I am a PhD researcher at the University of Oxford, based in the Reasoning with Machines Lab (OxRML) and supervised by Professor Adam Mahdi. I work on building, and better understanding, safer ways to automate scientific research. AI agents now search the literature, write code and run experiments. I want to know which parts of research we can hand over to them, and how to check their work.

To do this, I work on LLM evaluations, post-training and AI safety, often with multimodal and agentic systems. My research has featured at AI venues such as ICML, NeurIPS and EMNLP.

Currently, I am interested in:

  • Models that improve models. How AI agents go about training other models when they must balance several machine learning objectives at once, and how well they do it.
  • Discovery and integrity. Which stages of training decide a model's ethics. Whether models trained without ethical limits make better scientific discoveries, and whether a model could learn to act ethically in training but not at test time.
  • Uncertainty and conflicting evidence. How models represent uncertainty and weigh conflicting information, and how this shapes the conclusions they reach.
  • AI in research. Whether automating research helps or harms science overall. What balance of human and AI work is best, and what kind of feedback, in what form, gets the best results.

Research

Selected publications. * Equal contribution. Full list on Google Scholar. Click a figure for a short summary.

  1. Evaluating AI-based Scientific Knowledge Synthesis with Epidemiological Systematic Reviews

    S. Padarha*, R. O. Kearns*, T. Naidoo, L. Yang, Ł. Borchmann, P. Błaszczyk, C. Morgenstern, R. McCabe, S. Bhatia, P. H. Torr, J. Foerster, S. A. Hale, T. Rawson, A. Cori, E. Semenova, A. Mahdi

    NeurIPS 2026 Paper Code Dataset Website

    TL;DR We built AgentSLR, an open harness that runs each stage of an epidemiological systematic review with an LLM. We tested five frontier models against expert labels for 16,248 articles on WHO priority pathogens. No model led at every stage, and data extraction was the weakest step. The full pipeline took about 20 hours, against roughly 385 hours for a manual review.

    Key takeaway LLMs can cut review time sharply, but they are not yet reliable enough to work without expert supervision.

  2. Agentic Reinforcement Learning for Search Misaligns Instruction-Tuning

    Y. Yang, S. Padarha, S. Ball, A. Lee, A. Mahdi

    EMNLP 2026 Paper Code

    TL;DR Reinforcement learning (RL) that teaches models to use search tools weakens the safety they gained in instruction tuning. When prompted to search before they could refuse, RL-trained models ran chains of harmful searches. Search safety fell by up to 68.6 percent in Qwen models and 69.6 percent in Llama models. We traced the drop to one direction in the models' internal activations. Penalising that direction during training restored safety without hurting accuracy.

    Key takeaway Training models to use tools can quietly undo safety training, and a simple penalty during RL can fix it.

  3. Strategic Navigation or Stochastic Search? How Agents and Humans Reason Over Document Collections

    Ł. Borchmann, J. Van Landeghem, M. Turski, S. Padarha, R. O. Kearns, A. Mahdi, et al.

    ICML 2026 · Oral, top 0.5% Paper Code Dataset Leaderboard

    TL;DR We built MADQA, a benchmark of 2,250 human-written questions over 800 PDF documents, to test how agents and people search document collections. The best agents matched human accuracy. But they answered different questions correctly and relied on repeated searching rather than planning. They also fell almost 20 percent short of the best possible score, often stuck in loops.

    Key takeaway Agents reach human-level accuracy on document search through brute force, not strategy.

  4. Running the Gauntlet: Challenging Agentic Tasks

    M. Vysotskyi*, R. Lin*, G. Biziel, …, S. Padarha, …, A. Mahdi, V. Karpiv, P. Torr, A. Bibi

    Preprint 2026 Paper Code Website

    TL;DR GauntletBench tests agents on 135 visual tasks in five unfamiliar professional web apps, from a video editor to a circuit designer. At release in June 2026, the best agent completed 28.2 percent of tasks, while non-expert humans completed over 80 percent. Three months later, GPT-6 Astra scored 83.0 percent, the first agent to beat the human baseline.

    Key takeaway Agents are improving fast on unfamiliar tools, and benchmarks now go out of date within months.

  5. Evaluating LLM-as-a-Judge under Multilingual, Multimodal and Multi-domain Constraints

    S. Padarha, E. Semenova, B. Vidgen, A. Mahdi, S. A. Hale

    NeurIPS 2025 Workshop Paper

    TL;DR LLMs are widely used as judges during post-training, but had not been tested in settings that are both multilingual and multimodal. We built PolyVis, a benchmark in 12 languages covering hallucination detection, reasoning and safety, and tested four open-source judge models. Accuracy reached 95.2 percent on hallucination detection but stayed near chance on reasoning.

    Key takeaway No judge model is best everywhere. Performance depends on the mix of model, language and task, so evaluations should report each separately.

  6. Fully Automated Deep Learning Framework for Detection and Classification of Impacted Mandibular Third Molars in Panoramic Radiographs

    S. K. Veerabhadrappa, S. Vengusamy, S. Padarha, K. Iyer, S. Yadav

    Journal of Oral Medicine and Oral Surgery 2025 Paper

    TL;DR We built a fully automated system that finds impacted wisdom teeth in panoramic X-rays and classifies their position. It combines VGG16 for detection, YOLOv7 to locate the tooth and ResNet50 for classification. Working with clinicians in Malaysia and the Philippines, we trained it on about 1,800 X-rays checked by an oral radiologist. It detected the teeth with 93.5 percent accuracy and classified them with 92.2 percent accuracy.

    Key takeaway Deep learning can reliably automate a routine step in planning wisdom tooth surgery. Dental schools now use the system.
